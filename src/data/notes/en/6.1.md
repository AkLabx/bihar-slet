## 1. Origin and History of Bibliometrics

> **Definition (Alan Pritchard):** "Bibliometrics is the application of mathematical and statistical methods to books, articles, and other media of communication."

### Alan Pritchard and Wyndham Hulme:
- **Coinage of the Term:** The term **"Bibliometrics"** was coined in **1969** by British information scientist **Alan Pritchard** in his landmark paper titled *"Statistical Bibliography or Bibliometrics?"*, published in the *Journal of Documentation*.
- **Precursor — Statistical Bibliography:** Before Pritchard, **E. Wyndham Hulme** introduced the term **"Statistical Bibliography"** in **1923** during his lectures on the history of science and technology. Pritchard argued that Hulme's term was clumsy and lacked international clarity, replacing it with the cleaner term "Bibliometrics".

### The Metric Family Tree:
The quantitative evaluation of information and scientific communication encompasses several specialized, interrelated metric disciplines:

```
                                  [ Metric Studies Family ]
                                              |
      +-------------------+-------------------+-------------------+-------------------+
      |                   |                   |                   |                   |
[Bibliometrics]     [Scientometrics]    [Informetrics]      [Webometrics]       [Altmetrics]
Pritchard (1969)    Nalimov (1969)      Otto Nacke (1979)   Ingwersen (1997)    Priem (2010)
```

1. **Scientometrics:**
   - **Originators:** Coined in **1969** by Russian scholars **Vasily Nalimov** and Z. M. Mulchenko under the Russian term *"Naukometriya"*. (Prof. Derek J. de Solla Price also made monumental contributions to the quantitative study of science).
   - **Scope:** The quantitative and statistical study of the growth of science, scientific policies, institutional research outputs, and scientific communication structures.
2. **Informetrics:**
   - **Originator:** Coined in **1979** by German information scientist **Otto Nacke**.
   - **Scope:** The application of mathematical and statistical methods to investigate the properties of all forms of information—both recorded and unrecorded—covering production, dissemination, and utilization.
3. **Webometrics (Cybermetrics):**
   - **Originators:** Introduced in **1997** by **Tomas Almind** and **Peter Ingwersen**.
   - **Scope:** Quantitative analysis of the World Wide Web, including web page structures, hyperlink networks, search engine visibility, and the Web Impact Factor (WIF).
4. **Altmetrics (Alternative Metrics):**
   - **Originators:** Formulated in **2010** by **Jason Priem** and colleagues in the published *Altmetrics Manifesto*.
   - **Scope:** Real-time measurement of scholarly impact across the social web (X/Twitter, blogs, Mendeley, Wikipedia, policy briefs, and open-access downloads) as an immediate supplement to slow-accumulating traditional citations.

## 2. The Three Classical Empirical Laws of Bibliometrics

In library and information science, three foundational empirical laws govern the distribution of literature across journals, the productivity of scientific authors, and the frequency of words in textual documents:

```
                          The 3 Classical Bibliometric Laws
                                          |
      +-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
      |                                   |                                   |
1. Bradford's Law of Scattering      2. Lotka's Law of Productivity      3. Zipf's Law of Word Frequency
- Year: 1934                         - Year: 1926                        - Year: 1949
- Domain: Journal literature spread  - Domain: Scientific authorship     - Domain: Word occurrences in text
- Formula: 1 : n : n^2               - Formula: x^n . y = C (n ~ 2)       - Formula: r . f = C (Rank x Freq)
```

---

### (A) Bradford's Law of Scattering (1934)
- **Formulator:** **Samuel Clement Bradford**, Librarian at the Science Museum Library in London, in **1934** (republished in his 1948 seminal book *"Documentation"*).
- **Core Principle:** If scientific journals are arranged in decreasing order of productivity of articles on a specific discipline, the journals can be partitioned into a nucleus (core zone) devoted to the subject and successive peripheral zones containing approximately the same total number of articles as the nucleus:
  - **Zone 1 (Core / Nucleus Zone):** A small, highly specialized cadre of elite journals publishing a large proportion of all articles on the subject.
  - **Zone 2 (Allied Zone):** A moderately larger number of general scientific journals containing an equivalent total volume of articles.
  - **Zone 3 (Peripheral Zone):** A very broad, dispersed cluster of peripheral journals containing scattered articles.
- **Mathematical Progression (Bradford's Multiplier):**
  $$\mathbf{1 : n : n^2}$$
  *(where $1$ represents the number of core journals in Zone 1, and $n$ is the Bradford multiplier constant).*
- **Practical Application in Libraries:** Serves as the primary theoretical framework for collection development and serials management, guiding librarians in identifying indispensable core journals to preserve when faced with budgetary subscription cuts.

---

### (B) Lotka's Law of Scientific Productivity (1926)
- **Formulator:** American statistician and actuary **Alfred J. Lotka** in **1926** in his paper *"The Frequency Distribution of Scientific Productivity"*.
- **Alternative Name:** **The Inverse Square Law of Scientific Productivity**.
- **Core Principle:** In any given scientific discipline, a very small percentage of prolific researchers author the vast majority of published papers, while the vast majority of researchers publish only a single paper.
- **Mathematical Formula:**
  $$\mathbf{x^n \cdot y = C} \quad \text{or} \quad \mathbf{y_x = \frac{C}{x^2}}$$
  *(where $x$ represents the number of publications, $y_x$ is the relative proportion of authors publishing $x$ papers, and typically the exponent $n \approx 2$).*
- **Empirical Deduction (The 60% Rule):**
  - If 100 authors publish exactly **1 paper** ($x = 1$),
  - Then authors publishing **2 papers** will number $\frac{100}{2^2} = \frac{100}{4} = \mathbf{25}$ authors.
  - Authors publishing **3 papers** will number $\frac{100}{3^2} = \frac{100}{9} \approx \mathbf{11}$ authors.
  - Authors publishing **10 papers** will number $\frac{100}{10^2} = \frac{100}{100} = \mathbf{1}$ author.
  > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **High-Yield Exam Takeaway:** According to Lotka's Inverse Square Law, approximately **60% (or 60.79%)** of all authors within a scientific discipline publish only **one single paper** during their entire academic career.

---

### (C) Zipf's Law of Word Frequency (1949)
- **Formulator:** Harvard University linguist **George Kingsley Zipf** in his 1949 work *"Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort"*.
- **Core Principle:** In any natural language text corpus, the frequency of occurrence of any given word ($f$) is inversely proportional to its frequency rank ($r$). The product of rank and frequency remains constant.
- **Mathematical Formula:**
  $$\mathbf{r \cdot f = C}$$
  *(where $r$ is the word's numerical rank in descending frequency, $f$ is its observed frequency, and $C$ is a constant value).*
- **Empirical Interpretation:** The most frequently occurring word (Rank 1) appears roughly **twice as often** as the second most frequent word (Rank 2), and **three times as often** as the third word (Rank 3).
- **Practical Application in Libraries:** Essential in automated indexing, digital text retrieval, search engine ranking, and natural language processing for filtering non-informative stop words (e.g., "the", "and", "in") to isolate high-value topical keywords.

## 3. Citation Analysis and Evaluative Research Metrics

Citation analysis operates on the foundational premise that when a research publication or academic journal is frequently cited by peer researchers, it reflects substantial intellectual influence, scholarly relevance, and quality.

### (A) Journal Impact Factor (JIF)
- **Originators:** Devised by **Dr. Eugene Garfield** and Irving H. Sher during the 1960s at the **Institute for Scientific Information (ISI)**.
- **Publication:** Released annually through the **Journal Citation Reports (JCR)**, currently published and indexed by **Clarivate Analytics**.
- **Definition:** The average number of citations received in a given calendar year by citable articles published in that journal during the preceding two calendar years.
- **Calculation Formula:**
  $$\text{JIF (2025)} = \frac{\text{Citations in 2025 to items published in 2023 and 2024}}{\text{Total citable items published in 2023 and 2024}}$$

### (B) The h-index
- **Originator:** Proposed in **2005** by Argentine-American physicist **Jorge E. Hirsch** at the University of California, San Diego.
- **Definition:** A researcher has an **h-index of 'h'** if $h$ of their total $N_p$ published papers have each received **at least $h$ citations**, and the remaining $(N_p - h)$ papers have received no more than $h$ citations each.
- *Calculation Example:* Consider an author whose 7 published papers are ordered by descending citations:
  - Paper 1: 45 citations
  - Paper 2: 25 citations
  - Paper 3: 15 citations
  - Paper 4: 10 citations
  - Paper 5: 6 citations
  - Paper 6: 4 citations
  - Paper 7: 2 citations
  - Here, 5 papers have received 6 or more citations (each $\ge 5$), but the 6th paper has only 4 citations (which is $< 6$). Therefore, this researcher's **h-index = 5**.

### (C) The g-index
- **Originator:** Proposed in **2006** by Belgian informetrician **Leo Egghe** as an improvement over the h-index.
- **Distinction:** The standard h-index is insensitive to the citation count of an author's highly cited blockbuster papers. The g-index rewards highly cited papers by calculating the largest rank $g$ such that the top $g$ papers have cumulatively received at least $g^2$ citations ($g \ge h$).

### (D) The i10-index
- **Originator:** Introduced by **Google Scholar** in July **2011**.
- **Definition:** The total number of academic publications authored by a researcher that have each accumulated **at least 10 citations**.

## 4. Altmetrics (Alternative Metrics)

- **Concept:** Traditional citation metrics (such as the Journal Impact Factor and h-index) require 2 to 4 years to accumulate and become visible. In contrast, altmetrics track instantaneous digital attention on the web immediately following publication.
- **The Altmetric Donut:** A multi-colored circular badge reflecting diverse digital footprints:
  - Total mentions and shares on X (formerly Twitter) and Facebook.
  - Reader saves and bookmarks in reference managers such as Mendeley and Zotero.
  - Citations in Wikipedia articles.
  - Coverage in mainstream news outlets and government policy documents.
